Everything here is derived from these numbers

There is no structure that is right in the abstract. A trust that saves one family millions is, for the family next door, a five-figure annual bill for a benefit they were never going to receive. What separates the two is on this page: what you own, where you are taxed when you die, who is left, what can be taken from you, and how long the money has to grow before any of it matters.

This is a guide, not advice. Every threshold, rate and exemption in this app is a dated default you can edit, not a statement of current law — they move at every budget, and several of the big ones are legislated to move again. Nothing here is legal, tax or financial advice, no adviser–client relationship exists, and no structure described here should be created without qualified counsel in every jurisdiction where you, your assets or your heirs sit.

Jurisdiction

Fifteen regimes, each dated. Every figure is editable, because every one of them is a moving target.

How the regimes compare

Your estate, unchanged, taxed by each jurisdiction's default table. This is not an invitation to move: residence, domicile and situs rules mean the tax usually follows the asset or the person regardless of where the paperwork is filed.

Moving the growth, not the asset

The whole family of techniques below does one thing: it fixes the value of what you gave away at today's price and lets everything above that price accrue to someone else. They differ in how much of your exemption they consume, what happens if you die too early, and how hard they are to defend. They all fail in exactly one circumstance — the asset does not grow.

Compare the techniques on the same asset

GRAT — a zeroed-out annuity trust

You transfer an asset and take back an annuity whose present value equals what you put in, so the gift is nominally nothing. Whatever the asset earns above the statutory rate is left in the trust for your heirs, free of transfer tax.

Sale to a grantor trust

The same idea done with a note instead of an annuity: seed the trust, sell it the asset for a note at the minimum statutory interest rate, and let the spread do the work. Because the trust is ignored for income tax, you keep paying its tax bill — which is a further transfer that is not a gift.

Valuation discounts

A minority stake in a family entity is worth less than its share of the assets, because the holder cannot force a sale or a distribution. That is genuinely true and it is also the most heavily litigated number in the whole field.

The reason large fortunes are held the way they are is not secrecy. It is that you can separate the economics of an asset from the say over it, give away almost all of the first and keep almost all of the second. The limit on how far that goes is the point at which the tax authority decides you never really gave anything away.

Who holds the levers

Governance, in the order families build it

Charity, priced honestly

Giving is the one part of this field where the tax benefit is never the point, because you cannot end up richer by giving money away. What the structures buy you is control over timing, over the asset you give, and over who administers it afterwards — and the most expensive of them buys the least.

Foundation or fund

The same money, run two ways. A private foundation is a company you control; a donor-advised fund is an account at someone else's charity. The crossover is a real number and it is higher than most donors are told.

Split-interest trusts

Both cut the same asset in two along time: someone takes the income for a term and someone else takes what is left. Which half goes to charity is the only difference, and it changes everything about who the structure is for.

The line, and the doctrines that find it

Everything in this app is lawful planning, and lawful planning is not fragile: arranging your affairs to attract less tax is explicitly permitted, and has been for a century. What is not permitted is concealment — and the difference between the two is not the structure, it is whether the structure is real and whether it is reported. A trust you secretly control, an account you do not declare and a transfer you make once the claim has already arrived are not aggressive planning. They are the offence.
