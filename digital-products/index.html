Three criteria, with floors

The book's three tests for whether an idea is worth building. Scored 0–5 each — but not added up and averaged, because an average lets a strength hide a fatal weakness. Any single criterion at 0 or 1 kills the idea on its own, whatever the other two say. That is the point of a floor.

Score all three criteria below.

Why a floor and not an average

An idea that solves a real recurring problem for a buyer you cannot reach is not a three-out-of-five idea; it is a zero, because you will never sell it. An idea with a reachable buyer and no problem is also a zero, because they won't buy it. Averaging turns both into a respectable middling score and gets the product built.

So the verdict here is the lowest criterion first, and the total only after. A 5 and a 5 and a 1 is a kill. It is meant to feel unfair.

The 30-Minute Validation Method

Six five-minute blocks, run against a clock, each one asking for evidence rather than an opinion. Two of them are blocking: pass everything else and fail those, and the answer is still no. Thirty minutes is not enough to be sure. It is enough to stop being wrong for free.

What a pass here is not

It is not proof of demand. Six people saying they'd buy is six people being polite. The method's real output is the kill: it is cheap, fast, and reliably catches products whose buyer cannot be found or whose problem nobody is paying to solve. It cannot tell you a product will sell. It can tell you this one won't.

The case study in the source chapter is Denise Okafor, whose result from this method was to abandon what she was about to build. That is the method working, not failing.

The distribution nobody prices in

Every number on this page is a conditional: what happens if the product sells at the conversion rate you typed. The unconditional picture is different and worth saying plainly — across marketplaces, the earnings distribution for digital products is severely skewed. A small minority of listings take most of the revenue; the median listing earns close to nothing, and a large share of listings never make a single sale.

This is not a reason to skip it. It is the reason the validation tab exists and comes first: when most attempts earn nothing, the only move that changes your odds is finding out cheaply, before the eleven hours, which attempt you're making.

No figures are quoted here. The shape of that distribution is well established; the exact percentages differ by marketplace and year, and this app runs offline with no way to check a number it would be printing from memory. The shape is the part that should change your behaviour anyway.
