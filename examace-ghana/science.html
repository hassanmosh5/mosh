From the marking room

Three habits cost Science candidates more marks than any gap in knowledge. Unit conversion done carelessly — g/cm³ handed in when the paper asked for kg/m³. Diagrams drawn beautifully but labelled with arrows that touch nothing in particular. And the word “because” missing: an observation written down with no explanation attached to it earns half of what it could.

Step-by-step solutions

Every question opens up like this

The calculation and the reasoning, separated out — because in Science the marker wants to see both.

Integrated ScienceDensity & measurementHigh frequency

A metal block of mass 240 g is lowered into a measuring cylinder and displaces 30 cm³ of water. (a) Calculate the density of the metal in kg/m³. (b) State, with a reason, whether the block will float in water.

  1. Write the relationship before you write any numbers. One line, always the same, and it is worth a mark on its own. density = mass ÷ volume
  2. Read the unit the answer must be in — then convert first. The paper asked for kg/m³, so convert both quantities before dividing. Converting afterwards is where candidates slip. 240 g = 240 ÷ 1000 = 0.24 kg 30 cm³ = 30 ÷ 1 000 000 = 0.00003 m³
  3. Substitute and evaluate. density = 0.24 ÷ 0.00003 = 8000 kg/m³
  4. For part (b), compare — don’t guess. Floating is decided by comparing the object’s density with the density of the liquid. Water is 1000 kg/m³. 8000 kg/m³ > 1000 kg/m³
  5. Write the conclusion with the reason attached. “No” on its own scores nothing. The reason is the answer. The block will sink, because its density (8000 kg/m³) is greater than the density of water (1000 kg/m³). Examiner’s note: whenever a Science question says “state, with a reason” or “explain”, there are two marks in it — one for the statement and one for the reason. Answering only the first half is the single most common way to score 1 out of 2 across a whole paper.

Where the marks were: the formula, the conversion, the substitution, the comparison, and the reasoned conclusion. Five marks, and only one of them is arithmetic.
