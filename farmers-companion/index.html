The farm as it actually is

Everything this app computes comes from what you enter on this page: the weather your season has to live inside, the plots you can plant, the water you can actually deliver, the hands available in the week everything needs doing at once, and the money you have before the first sale. Nothing here is fetched from anywhere — there is no network connection in this file — so the climate figures are typical long-run averages you should overwrite with your own records wherever you have them.

Where the season stands

What each crop asks for, and what it gives back

Every crop below carries the handful of numbers that decide everything else: how long it occupies the ground, how much water it drinks, how much of each nutrient leaves the field inside the harvest, how many hours of work it demands and when, and how badly it suffers when it is short of water. The yields are what a well-run smallholding reaches in a good year with this crop — the ceiling, not the expectation. What you will actually get is worked out on the season plan, and it is lower.

Prices and ceilings are yours to set

The farmgate prices here are placeholders with no authority whatsoever — they are the one input nobody else can supply for you. Change them to what the buyer at your market paid last season, and change the yield ceiling to the best you have ever personally seen on your own soil.

Rotation families

Two crops from the same family, one after the other on the same plot, share their pests and their soil-borne diseases. The season plan flags it.

The plan, and the yield it can actually reach

Give each plot a crop and a planting date. The app does not ask you for a yield, because nobody can honestly type one: it starts from the crop's ceiling and multiplies it down by what the plan is short of — water, nutrients, protection, timing, seed. Every factor is shown with its working, and every one of them is something you can go and change.

The water balance, month by month

A crop's demand is not one number for the season: it is the reference evaporation for each month multiplied by where the crop is in its life. Against that sits the rain — not all of which is usable — and whatever you can carry or pump. What is left over is the deficit, and the deficit is converted into lost yield through the crop's own sensitivity, not a flat penalty.

What water is worth here

Feed the crop you are trying to grow, not the field

Fertiliser is bought in bags of a compound and applied by the handful, but crops take up nutrients in a fixed ratio per tonne of harvest. So the calculation runs the other way round: decide the yield you are aiming at, work out what that harvest removes, subtract what the soil and any manure supply, and only then find the cheapest combination of bags that covers what is left. A blend that is short on one nutrient is not a bit worse — it caps the yield at whatever that nutrient allows, and the extra kilos of the others are wasted money.

Where the soil is going

Removal against replacement, plot by plot. A field that gives up more than it gets back is being spent, not farmed.

Losses you can prevent, and the ones you will pay for anyway

Nothing here recommends a chemical. The things that reduce loss most on a smallholding cost no money at all — rotating the family, planting on time, walking the field once a week and knowing what you are looking at, pulling out the first infected plants, and keeping the store dry. Each practice you commit to takes a bite out of the loss the pests on your crops would otherwise take, and the arithmetic is shown. What is left after that is the loss the plan is carrying, and it is already inside every yield and margin in this app.

Scouting log

What you saw, on what date, and what you did about it. If you record a spray, the app takes its pre-harvest interval and checks it against the harvest date on the plan — the one mistake in this whole area that turns a good crop into an unsellable one.

Margin per hectare is the wrong ranking

It is the right one only if land is what you run out of. If the binding constraint is the week in February when everything needs weeding at once, then the crop to grow is the one that earns most per hour of that week — and it is often not the same crop. This page ranks both ways and says which ranking applies to your farm, then gives each crop the two numbers that matter when the season goes wrong: the yield below which it loses money, and the price below which it does.

Farms do not fail on the annual total

They fail in the month between the last of the fertiliser money and the first of the harvest money. A season with a good margin can still be impossible to finance, and the number that decides it is the deepest point of the cash curve — the working capital the plan actually needs, which is almost never the same as the total cost.
