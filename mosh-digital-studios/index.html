How a project runs

Five steps, and what each one costs you in time

The usual reason an automation project stalls is that the business owner turns out to be a dependency nobody budgeted for. So each step below says what we do, and what you have to do.

1

Configure, then talk

You open the tool for the service line you want and fill in your own reality — services, hours, prices, policies, the way you actually work. It takes fifteen to thirty minutes and it replaces a discovery questionnaire.

You: 20–30 minutes, alone, no meeting.
2

Scope and fixed price

Your configuration exports as a brief. We turn it into a scope with a fixed price, a list of what is explicitly not included, and the access we'll need. If the honest answer is that the automation won't pay for itself, that is what the scope says.

You: one 45-minute call, then a yes or a no.
3

Build against your configuration

The system gets built to the configuration you signed off, in a staging environment connected to test data. Nothing touches a live customer, a live calendar or a live payment until you have watched it work.

You: accounts and access, once. Then nothing for a week or two.
4

Watch it fail on purpose

Before go-live we run the failure cases with you at the screen: the double-booking attempt, the refund demand, the message at 2am, the customer who says something the system should refuse to handle. You approve the escalation rules by seeing them fire.

You: one 60-minute session. The most important hour of the project.
5

Handover, then care or leave

You get the runbook, the credentials, and a recorded walkthrough. The care plan is optional and cancellable monthly — a system you can't leave isn't a system, it's a hostage situation.

You: decide whether you want us next month.
What we won't do. We won't put an AI in front of a customer without an escalation path to a human, won't automate a process nobody has written down, and won't ship a system whose failure is silent. Each of those is a way of moving a problem rather than fixing it.

Questions

      
The ones worth answering honestly

    


    

      Will an AI answering my customers embarrass me?
      

        

          It can, and the way it happens is always the same: the system is given no way to say "I
          don't know", so it invents. That is why every conversational build here starts from a
          refusal list and an escalation path rather than a personality.
        

        

          The rule we hold to is that the assistant may only state things it can look up — a real
          slot from the real diary, a real price from the real list. Anything else it hands to a
          human. You approve those rules by watching them fire in step 4, before go-live.
        

      

    


    

      Are the prices on this page what I'll pay?
      

        

          No, and the page says so wherever a number appears. The rate card is a set of placeholder
          figures chosen so the arithmetic is legible; they are not a market survey. The estimator's
          job is to show you how a price is built — which lines, which multipliers, what
          discount — so that a real quote is something you can interrogate.
        

      

    


    

      What happens to the data I type into this site?
      

        

          It stays in this browser. This page makes no network requests of any kind: no fonts, no
          analytics, no CDN, no form endpoint. Your estimator selections and brief are saved to this
          browser's local storage so you can come back to them, and they can be wiped with the Clear
          button on the brief.
        

        

          The "Open in email" button hands the text to whatever mail client your device uses. That
          is the only moment anything leaves the page, and you are the one pressing it.
        

      

    


    

      Why is there no client list or testimonials?
      

        

          Because the usual versions are unverifiable, and a stranger cannot tell an earned logo
          from a decorative one. The alternative offered here is stronger and harder to fake:
          thirteen working tools you can open, use offline, and read the source of. If the
          engineering in them is good, that is evidence; if it isn't, no testimonial would have
          fixed it.
        

      

    


    

      What if the automation isn't worth it for my business?
      

        

          Then the scope should say so, and sometimes it does. The payback panel in the estimator is
          built to be able to return a bad answer: if your own figures don't recover the build cost
          in a sensible number of months, it prints that plainly rather than burying it.
        

        

          Small businesses under a certain volume of enquiries genuinely are better served by a
          cheap shared inbox and a rule about answering it. That is a legitimate outcome of a
          scoping call.
        

      

    


    

      Do I get locked in?
      

        

          The care plan is monthly and cancellable. At handover you receive the runbook, the
          credentials, and a recorded walkthrough — the things you'd need to take the system to
          somebody else. A system whose only documentation is the person who built it is a liability
          that happens to be working at the moment.
        

      

    


    

      Can I just use the tools and build it myself?
      

        

          Yes. They are complete, offline, and free to open. They were written to be genuinely
          useful on their own, not as a demo that stops at the interesting part. If a capable person
          in your business works through the relevant one and builds the thing, that is a good
          outcome and costs you nothing.
