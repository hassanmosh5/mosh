The factory

A plan to publish "multiple products every month" is a claim about hours, not about software. This tab works out how many products a month you can actually finish, and how big the catalogue gets before looking after it consumes the time you were going to build with. Everything else in the app feeds these numbers.

What this app argues

  1. A factory's output is set by its slowest human step, not its fastest AI one. Generation collapses drafting from days to minutes and leaves deciding, checking, formatting and answering customers exactly where they were. On the workflows in this app the generation steps are a minority of the clock — the Production tab totals both, so you can see the ratio rather than assume it.
  2. Every published product is a standing cost, not a finished asset. Support questions, refunds, a broken download link, a syllabus that changed, a model that no longer behaves the way the prompt pack says. Upkeep scales with the catalogue; your week doesn't. There is a catalogue size at which upkeep eats every build hour you have, and the simulation below finds it.
  3. The pipeline is exactly as automatic as its worst gate. Most steps here can run unattended. A handful cannot, because being wrong is expensive in a way no amount of review-by-another-model fixes — wrong physics in an exam guide, a feed ratio that kills birds, an invented regnal date in a history of a living institution. Those gates are marked, costed, and never checked off automatically.

What the simulation is not

  • Not a forecast of your sales. It multiplies your traffic guess by your conversion guess. Both are yours; the app supplies no market data and cannot.
  • Not a claim that products decay at 15% a month. That is a default you can change on the Economics tab. What is not a guess is the shape: an unmarketed product falls towards a floor rather than holding its launch month.
  • Not an argument against publishing. It is an argument for choosing the catalogue size on purpose instead of discovering it when the support inbox stops fitting into a Saturday.

Ideas, ranked

Twelve seeded from the studio's own list, scored on five things, ranked by the product of what they earn and what they cost you to make. Commit the ones you intend to build — the committed set is what the Production, Economics and Schedule tabs work on.

How the score is built

Five factors, each 0–10, each weighted. The weights are the app's judgement and yours to argue with; change them and every rank below moves.

Rank is not score. Score says how good the product is; rank divides it by the hours the workflow says it takes, because a studio with one pair of hands is choosing between products, not collecting them. A merely-good ebook beats a slightly-better course whenever the course costs three times the week.

Production

Three workflows — ebook, course, template pack — broken into the steps that actually consume a day, each with the agent that runs it, the artifact it must produce before the next step starts, and how long it takes you rather than the model. Steps with a gate cannot be delegated; they are why the pipeline is not a button.

The seven agents

Each one is a system prompt, written from the product you have selected, with your own facts substituted and the things the model must not invent stated as prohibitions. Copy one into a project, a custom GPT, or the top of a chat. They are written to be pasted once and reused, not retyped per message.

Briefing for

The prohibitions are the useful half. A model asked for a testimonial writes one; a model asked for a statistic supplies one. Every brief below ends with a list of things it must leave as a marked hole for you to fill, because a hole is visible and an invention is not.

The checks a model can't do for you

Category by category: what generated content in this subject gets wrong, how it shows up after you have sold it, and who can actually settle it. These are the gates costed on the Production tab. Nothing here is a general worry about AI — each one is specific to something on the studio's own product list.

The refund arithmetic behind the gates

A gate costs a fixed number of minutes every time. A missed gate costs refunds, the support thread each refund brings, and the reviews that follow — and unlike the gate, that bill arrives after the money has been spent and scales with how well the product sold. The arithmetic above uses your own upkeep and refund figures.

Two currencies, one price

The app has no exchange rate and never fetches one. A price that reads as reasonable in one currency and absurd in the other is the commonest way a two-store setup loses money — not through fees, through a price nobody local will pay.

The funnel, priced

Seven steps from a visitor to a coaching client. Type the rates you believe; the app multiplies them out per thousand visitors and says which step the money actually comes from. It is very rarely the one that gets the most attention.

Rates and prices

Each rate is of the step above it, not of the original visitors — 3% of buyers taking the core product is a very different number from 3% of everybody who landed. Compounding is where funnel arithmetic usually goes wrong in a spreadsheet.

What this factory deliberately does not do

    
    
      
  • It does not research the market. Every demand and competition score is a
        judgement you typed. The app makes them explicit and weighs them consistently; it cannot
        make them true.
    • 
      
  • It does not publish anything. No store, no payment processor, no mailing list,
        no network of any kind. It works out what to publish and what it will earn; the
        uploading is yours.
    • 
      
  • It does not write the product. It writes the briefs that write the product,
        and it is explicit about which parts of the result a person still has to verify.
    • 
      
  • It does not promise a month of output. It computes one from your hours and
        then tells you when the number you wanted is not in them.
