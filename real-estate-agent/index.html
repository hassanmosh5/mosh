The numbers behind a deal

Your hourly figure is an opportunity cost, not a wage. It is what the hour would have earned doing the work only you can do — the listing appointment, the negotiation. Most agents who compute it honestly land somewhere well above what they would pay an assistant, which is the whole argument for handing the first reply to something that never sleeps.

A verdict per source, because “leads” is not one thing

A portal enquiry and a past-client referral are both called a lead and share almost nothing else: they differ by a factor of fifteen in how often they close and by a factor of thirty in what they cost. Blend them into one number and every decision you make afterwards is wrong. So the unit here is the source, each with its own funnel, its own cost, and its own answer to the only question that matters — what does one closing from here cost you, in money and in Saturdays.

What the verdicts mean

The rates are the app's, not the industry's

Every starting conversion rate on this tab is a number this app applies, printed where you can see and change it. None of them is a benchmark, and no benchmark would fit your market anyway.

The rate to distrust most is close — the share of signed agreements that become completed transactions. It carries fall-through, financing that collapses, surveys that come back badly and clients who change their minds. If your market has a number for it, it is the first one to overwrite.

The second is cost per lead for anything you are not billed for. Prospecting is not free because no invoice arrives: the ledger prices your hours at the rate on the Practice tab and puts them in the cost per deal, which is why the cheapest sources on paper are rarely the cheapest here.

The cheapest lead you will ever buy is the one you already paid for

Nothing else in this app moves the numbers like the two figures on this tab: how long a new enquiry waits for a reply, and how many times you try before you stop. Both are usually worse than the agent believes, and both are the part an assistant that never sleeps actually fixes. This is the tab that pays for the other ten.

The curve this app applies

Reaching a new enquiry gets harder with every minute it waits. The shape below is the app's, stated so you can argue with it: a reply inside a minute is the baseline, and everything else is a fraction of it.

Each source also carries a sensitivity. A portal enquiry sent to four agents at once is decided almost entirely by who answers first, so it takes the full penalty. A referral from a client you sold for in 2019 will wait until Monday, so it barely notices. The applied penalty is 1 − sensitivity × (1 − curve).

And how many times you try

The second curve: the share of the reachable ceiling you actually reach, by number of attempts across the whole follow-up window. Most agents stop at two. Most contacts happen after that.

The weakest assumption in this app is that these two multiply. Contact rate here is ceiling × speed × attempts, which treats replying fast and following up often as independent — and they are not: some of the people you reach on the sixth attempt are the same people a two-minute reply would have caught first time. So the combined figure is the optimistic end. If you want the cautious version, hold the assistant's attempts at the number you already manage and let it improve only the reply time; the gain that remains is the one nobody can argue with.

What the two changes are worth, source by source

Extra deals a year, and the commission attached to them, from answering faster and following up longer. Nothing here assumes a better agent, a better market or a better lead — only a quicker reply.

Out of hours is not an edge case

Four questions, asked in the first ten minutes, in the right order

Qualifying is not an interrogation and it is not a form. It is a short, ordered set of questions whose answers change what happens next — and a rule, written down in advance, for what each combination of answers means. Write the rule here and the assistant applies it identically at 3pm and at 3am. Leave it unwritten and it will invent one.

The tracks

Where the line sits

The score is the weighted average of the answers, out of 100. Two thresholds turn it into an action, and they are yours: move them and the routing on every lead moves with them.

Try it on a lead

Answer as a real enquiry would and watch the rubric decide. This is the same arithmetic the brief hands the model, so if the verdict here looks wrong, the weights above are wrong.

The answers that end the conversation early

Some answers are not a low score, they are a stop. Each of these overrides the number and sends the lead somewhere else — usually to a person, sometimes politely away.

Questions it must never ask

A qualification script is the easiest place in a property business to commit a fair-housing offence, because the unlawful questions sound like small talk. These are fixed, not preferences, and the Fair housing tab explains each one.

The part that is not a preference

Everywhere else in this app you are choosing. Here you are not. An assistant that answers property enquiries can break housing discrimination law in a single friendly sentence, and it will do it in the exact register a well-meaning agent does: by being helpful about which sort of person lives where. The clauses below are written as absolutes, most of them cannot be switched off, and they go into the brief verbatim.

Where you practise

This is a working list, not legal advice. Protected characteristics are added locally all the time — source of income, occupation, criminal record, immigration status, student status — and a city ordinance can be stricter than the national statute above it. Have someone who is qualified in your jurisdiction read the brief before it answers a single message. The app's job is to make sure there is something specific for them to read.

Never, under any wording

Locked clauses cannot be turned off, because each one is the thing an assistant does wrong by default: it wants to be helpful, and the helpful answer is the unlawful one.

The question you will actually get

Some version of “is it a nice area?” arrives in almost every buyer conversation. It is not a trap and the buyer is not testing you — but every warm, natural answer to it is steering. So the answer is scripted, and the script is the same every time.

The arithmetic, with the awkward terms left in

      it usually means your lead volume is too low for automation to matter yet.
