What the agent is for, and how much work there is

A support agent is not a chatbot with your FAQ pasted into it. It is a member of staff with a job description, a set of things it is allowed to decide, and a manager it hands the rest to. Start with the business and the workload — every other tab is computed from what you enter here.

The workload

Loaded cost is what an hour of support actually costs you: salary, employer taxes, software seats, management time and the hours they are paid but not answering. For most small teams it lands 25–60% above the hourly wage. If you outsource, use the price you pay.
Used in one place only: the cost of an answer bad enough to lose the customer. It is the term that decides whether automating an intent is worth it, so a guess here is a guess in the headline number.

What that adds up to

Decide intent by intent, because “support” is not one job

Automating support as a whole is a decision nobody can make well. Automating “where is my order” is a decision anyone can make in ten seconds. So the unit here is the intent: what the message is asking for. Each one gets its own verdict — the agent resolves it, drafts a reply for a human, or hands it straight over.

You cannot automate what you have not written down

Every intent needs specific facts, and an agent that lacks them does not fall silent — it invents something plausible. This tab tracks what each intent needs and blocks the ones whose knowledge is missing. That is the whole safety mechanism, and it is deliberately annoying.

Things to write down

Things it has to look up

Live data the agent cannot know from a document — an order, a booking, an account. Read means it can look the answer up. Act means it can change something, which is a different risk class and a different conversation with whoever owns that system.

What a knowledge source has to be

Written

  • Answers the question a customer asks, in the words they ask it — not the words your policy uses.
  • States the exceptions. “30-day returns” is not a policy; the four cases where it does not apply are the policy.
  • Carries a date and an owner. Anything older than the last price change is a liability.
  • Contains no internal-only note that would embarrass you if quoted verbatim, because it will be.

Partial

  • It exists but is out of date, incomplete, scattered across three documents, or lives in one person's head and a Slack thread.
  • Counted at three-quarters weight, which is generous.
  • The usual cause of an agent that is right about the common case and confidently wrong about the exception.

What it may decide, and where it stops

An agent with no stated limits has one limit anyway — whatever the model felt like doing that time. These settings become hard clauses in the generated brief, and several of them reach back and force an intent to a human whatever you chose on the Intents tab.

Money it may commit you to

Identity

Before the agent reveals or changes anything account-specific.

When it is not sure

The single most valuable behaviour to specify, and the one every generic setup leaves out.

Whatever you pick, the brief forbids the agent from stating a policy, price, date or figure it cannot find in its sources. Not discourages — forbids, with the fallback sentence written out for it, because a model with no scripted way to say “I don't know” will always find something else to say.

Hand it to a human when…

Triggers checked mid-conversation, on top of whatever the intent was routed as.

Never, under any wording

These go in the brief as absolute clauses. Five of them cannot be turned off, and the reason is written next to each.

Rules these follow

        
    
          
  • No apology inflation. One apology, at the start, only where something actually went wrong. Repeating it reads as evasion.
    • 
          
  • Say the thing first. The answer or the decision is the first sentence; the reasoning comes after it, for the people who want it.
    • 
          
  • Never promise a date the business has not agreed to. Ranges come from the knowledge sources or they do not appear.
    • 
          
  • One next step. A reply offering three options is a reply that generates another message.
    • 
          
  • A variable it cannot fill is a handoff, not a gap left in the sent message.

How often it has to be right before it saves you anything

    

      The usual case for a support agent counts the tickets it answers. This one also counts the tickets it
      answers wrongly, because those do not cost zero — the customer comes back crosser, a
      person untangles it, and some of them leave. Below a certain accuracy an automated intent costs more
      than the person it replaced, and that threshold is different for every intent.

What this does not count

      
    
        
  • Faster replies. Answering at 3am has real value and no invoice, so it is reported as hours of waiting removed rather than money.
    • 
        
  • The tickets you never see. A good agent absorbs questions people would otherwise have abandoned along with the purchase. That is upside this refuses to guess at.
    • 
        
  • Your time reading transcripts. Budget it in upkeep hours; the first month is heavier than every month after.
    • 
        
  • Redundancies. Hours saved is not headcount saved unless someone actually leaves, and a support team at capacity converts saved hours into faster replies instead. Both are fine; only one shows up in the bank.
    • 
        
  • Seasonality. One monthly volume figure, evenly spread. If you have a peak, run the numbers twice.
    •
